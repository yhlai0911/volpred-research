\begin{abstract}
This paper proposes the Periodic Realized GARCH (PRG) model, which maintains a single GARCH variance recursion while allowing session-specific parameters for overnight and intraday periods. The key mechanism is that the conditional variance $h_{n-1}$ from one session carries into the next, creating an information bridge across session boundaries. Using tick-level data from Taiwan futures (TAIFEX), daily OHLC data from four U.S. exchange-traded funds (SPY, QQQ, GLD, EEM), and the Taiwan 0050 ETF, the PRG model significantly outperforms GJR-GARCH in all six markets under the QLIKE loss function, with Diebold--Mariano test statistics of 6.63 (EEM), 6.12 (GLD), 6.00 (SPY), 5.27 (0050.TW), 5.10 (TAIFEX), and 4.26 (QQQ)---all exceeding the \citet{Harvey2016} threshold of $|t|>3.0$. An ablation removing the session-boundary update collapses the advantage (DM $t = 6.00 \to -0.57$), and a fair-information GJR-X benchmark on SPY confirms PRG's dominance is structural, not informational (PRG vs GJR-X DM $t = 7.72$; GJR-X vs GJR DM $t = -0.53$, NS). PRG also dominates in VaR and Expected Shortfall evaluations and yields economic gains in TAIFEX volatility timing.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Keywords:} Volatility forecasting; Periodic GARCH; Session decomposition; Overnight variance; Cross-session information transfer

\medskip\noindent

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Financial markets operate across distinct trading sessions---an overnight period when exchanges are closed yet information arrives continuously, and an intraday period characterized by active price discovery. \citet{Blanc2014} demonstrate that overnight and intraday returns exhibit fundamentally different volatility feedback structures: past intraday returns affect both future sessions symmetrically, while past overnight returns primarily feed back into future overnight volatility. This asymmetry motivates session-specific modeling, yet the standard approach treats close-to-close returns as a single process, discarding the information embedded in session boundaries.

Several approaches have been proposed to exploit session-level information. \citet{Bollerslev1996} introduce periodic structures into GARCH models for calendar-based variation. \citet{Linton2020} develop a coupled component DCS-EGARCH model that allows cross-session feedback with approximately twelve parameters. \citet{Kim2023} propose an Overnight GARCH-It\^{o} model using continuous-time diffusions for each session. \citet{Todorova2014} show that treating overnight information separately improves realized volatility forecasts, while \citet{Opschoor2021} apply score-driven models to realized variances with overnight returns for VaR and ES forecasting. These models are theoretically rich but typically require complex estimation procedures, limiting practitioner adoption. The PRG framework proposed in this paper takes a different route: we extend the periodic GARCH structure of \citet{Bollerslev1996} from calendar periodicity (e.g., day-of-week) to session-frequency periodicity (overnight versus intraday), retaining its parsimony while embedding a single cross-session variance recursion.

\citet{Lai2024} address the session decomposition problem with the Periodic Regime-Switching (PRS) model, which uses Markov switching to capture session-specific dynamics. However, the PRS model's regime-switching mechanism introduces substantial estimation complexity and raises concerns about overfitting in moderate samples. This paper proposes a parsimonious alternative---the Periodic Realized GARCH (PRG)---that retains the key insight of session-specific parameters while eliminating the Markov-switching apparatus entirely.

The PRG model makes three contributions. First, it introduces a single GARCH recursion with session-periodic parameters (6--8 total), where the conditional variance from one session carries directly into the next---creating a \emph{session-boundary information transfer}. This parsimonious specification stands in deliberate contrast to existing session-aware models requiring substantially more parameters: \citet{Bollerslev1996}'s original calendar-based periodic GARCH and its session-extended variants, coupled DCS-EGARCH systems with approximately twelve parameters \citep{Linton2020}, continuous-time Overnight GARCH-It\^{o} diffusions \citep{Kim2023}, and Markov regime-switching mechanisms \citep{Lai2024}---each of which complicates estimation and risks overfitting in moderate samples. Second, it develops a fair comparison framework following \citet{Hansen2005} and \citet{Patton2011}: all forecasts are converted to a common target ($\sigma^2_{\text{full}} = r^2_{\text{overnight}} + r^2_{\text{intraday}}$), revealing that previously documented HAR dominance over GJR is largely a target-mismatch artifact (DM $t = 0.57$ on the common target)---an evaluation methodology bias systematically obscured in prior literature. Third, it provides cross-asset evidence spanning six markets, all surpassing the \citet{Harvey2016} threshold of $|t| > 3.0$.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Session decomposition}

Each trading day $d$ is decomposed into two sessions indexed by $s \in \{0, 1\}$, where $s = 0$ denotes the overnight session and $s = 1$ denotes the intraday session. Let $P^{c}_d$ and $P^{o}_d$ denote the closing and opening prices on day $d$. The session returns are
\begin{align}
    r_{d,0} &= \log(P^{o}_d / P^{c}_{d-1}), \label{eq:r_overnight} \\
    r_{d,1} &= \log(P^{c}_d / P^{o}_d). \label{eq:r_intraday}
\end{align}
For markets with tick-level data (e.g., TAIFEX), $r^2_{d,s}$ can be replaced by 5-minute realized variance $\text{RV}_{d,s}$ computed within each session. For markets with only daily OHLC data, squared returns $r^2_{d,s}$ serve as unbiased (albeit noisy) proxies for session-level variance.

The common evaluation target for all models is the full-day variance:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^2_{\text{full},d} = r^2_{d,0} + r^2_{d,1},
    \label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
which is an unbiased estimator of $\text{Var}(r_{d,c2c})$ under the assumption that overnight and intraday returns are uncorrelated. Under QLIKE, model rankings are invariant to the choice of unbiased proxy \citep{Patton2011, Hansen2005}; this robustness result originates in \citet{Patton2011} for robust forecast-loss functions, with \citet{Hansen2005} establishing the companion proxy-substitution property.

\subsection{The PRG model}

The Periodic Realized GARCH operates a single variance recursion that alternates between session-specific parameter sets. Index observations sequentially as $n = 1, 2, \ldots, 2D$ (two sessions per day for $D$ days), with session indicator $s_n \in \{0, 1\}$. Let $r_n$ denote the return in observation $n$ and $x_n = r^2_n$ the volatility proxy. The PRG Basic specification is
\begin{equation}
    h_n = \omega_{s_n} + \alpha_{s_n} \, x_{n-1} + \beta_{s_n} \, h_{n-1},
    \label{eq:prg_basic}
\end{equation}
where $h_n = E[r^2_n \mid \mathcal{F}_{n-1}]$ is the conditional variance for session $n$, and $(\omega_s, \alpha_s, \beta_s)$ are session-specific parameters. The label ``Realized'' here denotes that the session-frequency squared return $x_n = r^2_n$ is a realized (observed) volatility proxy entering the recursion, distinct from the daily Realized GARCH framework of \citet{Hansen2012} that augments a GARCH equation with a high-frequency realized variance measurement equation. The information set $\mathcal{F}_{n-1} = \sigma(r_1, \ldots, r_{n-1})$ contains all returns from sessions that have \emph{completed} before session $n$ begins. Crucially, sessions are temporally ordered: the overnight session closes before the intraday session opens. When $n$ indexes an intraday session, $\mathcal{F}_{n-1}$ includes the just-completed overnight return $r_{n-1}$, so $x_{n-1} = r^2_{n-1}$ is a realized quantity---not a forecast. This session-by-session conditioning is what creates a cross-session information bridge with only 6 parameters total. We assume $r_n \mid \mathcal{F}_{n-1} \sim N(0, h_n)$; all parameters are estimated by maximizing the Gaussian quasi-log-likelihood, which produces consistent estimates even under non-Gaussian innovations \citep{BollerslevWooldridge1992}.

The PRG Extended specification adds a leverage term:
\begin{equation}
    h_n = \omega_{s_n} + \alpha_{s_n} \, x_{n-1} + \gamma_{s_n} \, x_{n-1} \, \mathbb{1}(r_{n-1} < 0) + \beta_{s_n} \, h_{n-1},
    \label{eq:prg_extended}
\end{equation}
with session-specific leverage parameters $\gamma_s$, yielding 8 parameters. Let $\rho_s = \alpha_s + 0.5\gamma_s + \beta_s$ denote the persistence of session $s$ under the Extended specification; for the Basic specification (no leverage term) this simplifies to $\rho_s = \alpha_s + \beta_s$, recovering the standard GARCH(1,1) persistence under session-specific parameterization. Since the PRG alternates between two sessions in a deterministic cycle, the covariance stationarity condition follows \citet{Bollerslev1996}:
\begin{equation}
    \rho_0 \rho_1 < 1,
    \label{eq:stationarity}
\end{equation}
which requires only that the \emph{product} of session-specific persistences be less than unity. This is weaker than imposing $\rho_s < 1$ for each session individually: one session may exhibit transient explosiveness ($\rho_s > 1$) provided the other compensates. The session-specific unconditional variances are
\begin{equation}
    \bar{h}_s = \frac{\omega_s + \rho_s \, \omega_{1-s}}{1 - \rho_0 \rho_1}, \quad s \in \{0, 1\}.
    \label{eq:uncond_var}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Forecast timing and information sets.}
The session ordering dictates a two-phase forecasting protocol for full-day variance. Let $\mathcal{F}_{d-1}^{\,c}$ denote the information set available at the close of day $d-1$ (after the intraday session ends) and $\mathcal{F}_{d}^{\,o}$ the information set available at the open of day $d$ (after the overnight session closes). Because the overnight session spans the interval from the day-$(d-1)$ close to the day-$d$ open, the overnight return $r_{d,0}$ is realized---not forecasted---by the time $\mathcal{F}_{d}^{\,o}$ is formed. The PRG therefore produces two sequential session-level forecasts:
\begin{align}
    \hat{h}_{d,0} &= \mathbb{E}\!\left[r^2_{d,0} \mid \mathcal{F}_{d-1}^{\,c}\right]
    = \omega_{0} + \alpha_{0}\, r^2_{d-1,1} + \gamma_{0}\, r^2_{d-1,1}\, \mathbb{1}(r_{d-1,1} < 0) + \beta_{0}\, h_{d-1,1},
    \label{eq:prg_ov_forecast} \\[2pt]
    \hat{h}_{d,1} &= \mathbb{E}\!\left[r^2_{d,1} \mid \mathcal{F}_{d}^{\,o}\right]
    = \omega_{1} + \alpha_{1}\, r^2_{d,0} + \gamma_{1}\, r^2_{d,0}\, \mathbb{1}(r_{d,0} < 0) + \beta_{1}\, \hat{h}_{d,0}.
    \label{eq:prg_in_forecast}
\end{align}
Equation~(\ref{eq:prg_ov_forecast}) is a standard one-step-ahead forecast issued at the day-$(d-1)$ close. Equation~(\ref{eq:prg_in_forecast}) is a \emph{conditional} forecast issued at the day-$d$ open: the input $r^2_{d,0}$ is the realized (not forecasted) overnight squared return and is therefore an element of $\mathcal{F}_{d}^{\,o}$. This timing convention is standard in the session-based volatility literature \citep{Linton2020,Opschoor2021} and mirrors how overnight information is exploited in practice: a market participant observes the realized overnight return at the day-$d$ open and adjusts risk exposure or initiates positions at the opening price accordingly. Acting at the open on fully realized overnight information is a legitimate and routinely implementable timing convention, not a look-ahead construct.

Practical implementation requires only two conditions: (i) the overnight return is observed at the session boundary, and (ii) the intraday forecast update is computed before the first tradable opening price. Both conditions are routinely satisfied in modern exchange infrastructure, where opening prints are published in sub-milliseconds and standard volatility-targeting systems complete the update within the opening auction window. The timing protocol is therefore directly implementable in live risk-management workflows without specialized execution technology. The full-day forecast is the sum
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{full},d} = \hat{h}_{d,0} + \hat{h}_{d,1},
    \label{eq:prg_fullday}
\end{equation}
which is \emph{not} a single-timepoint forecast issued at $\mathcal{F}_{d-1}^{\,c}$ but rather the sum of two conditional forecasts issued at the two session boundaries. All benchmark models in Section~2.3 that target $\sigma^2_{\text{full},d}$ directly (GJR, HAR) are evaluated under the \emph{more restrictive} information set $\mathcal{F}_{d-1}^{\,c}$; the PRG's advantage under the two-phase protocol therefore captures, in addition to functional-form differences, the practical value of acting on overnight information at the open.

\subsection{Benchmark models}

We compare PRG against four alternatives. (i) \textbf{GJR-GARCH(1,1)} \citep{Glosten1993} estimated on close-to-close returns with Student-$t$ innovations; its forecast $\hat{\sigma}^2_{c2c}$ targets $\sigma^2_{\text{full}}$ directly. (ii) \textbf{HAR} \citep{Corsi2009}, estimated in log-space with daily, weekly, and monthly lags of $\sigma^2_{\text{full}}$; forecasts are converted to levels via the standard smearing correction for log-linear models. (iii) \textbf{Separate GJR}: independent GJR-GARCH(1,1) models for each session with leverage terms but no cross-session linkage, whose forecasts are summed as $\hat{h}_{d,0} + \hat{h}_{d,1}$. This serves as a critical ablation benchmark---the difference between PRG and Separate GARCH isolates the value of the cross-session information bridge.

All models are estimated out-of-sample with periodic refitting: GJR and HAR every 63 trading days, PRG and Separate GARCH every 126--252 days (reflecting higher estimation cost).

\subsection{Evaluation framework}

Statistical evaluation employs the QLIKE loss function, which \citet{Patton2011} shows is robust to noise in unbiased volatility proxies. Pairwise comparisons use the \citet{Diebold1995} test with \citet{Harvey1997} small-sample correction, applying the $|t| > 3.0$ threshold of \citet{Harvey2016}. The Model Confidence Set \citep{Hansen2011MCS} provides a joint assessment. Additional layers include Spearman rank correlations, VaR backtesting (\citealp{Kupiec1995}; \citealp{Christoffersen1998}; Basel traffic light), and Expected Shortfall evaluation via the \citet{Fissler2016} joint loss.

The PRG Extended model significantly outperforms GJR-GARCH in every market, with DM $t$-statistics ranging from 4.26 (QQQ) to 6.63 (EEM), all surpassing the \citet{Harvey2016} threshold of $|t|>3.0$. We adopt the \citet{Harvey2016} criterion---rather than the conventional $|t|>1.96$---because it explicitly corrects for the inflated false-positive rate that arises when multiple volatility forecasting models are compared on the same data set; this threshold is now standard in the model-confidence-set and volatility-forecasting literatures and provides a more conservative inference benchmark for our six-market comparison. The comparison against Separate GARCH is equally decisive: DM statistics range from $-4.07$ (TAIFEX) to $-6.69$ (SPY), confirming that the cross-session information bridge---not merely the session-specific parameterization---drives the improvement. The PRG Extended also significantly outperforms HAR in four of six markets; the TAIFEX comparison yields $t = 2.63$, below the Harvey threshold, a result we discuss further below.

The TAIFEX MCS analysis is particularly informative: at the 10\% significance level, only the PRG Basic and PRG Extended survive, with both GJR-GARCH ($p = 0.000$) and HAR ($p = 0.000$) eliminated. This is the strongest possible model selection outcome. For the U.S. ETFs, all models survive the MCS, reflecting the noisier daily OHLC proxies.

A notable finding concerns the GJR--HAR comparison. On the common $\sigma^2_{\text{full}}$ target, GJR and HAR are statistically indistinguishable on TAIFEX (DM $t = 0.57$). This stands in sharp contrast to results obtained when each model is evaluated on its native target, where HAR appears to dominate. The discrepancy illustrates the target-mismatch problem identified by \citet{Patton2011}: comparing models on different targets can produce spurious rankings.

The result is striking: removing the session-boundary update causes QLIKE to deteriorate from 0.748 to 0.864, and the DM statistic against GJR collapses from $6.00$ to $-0.57$---a swing of $6.57$ standard deviations. The ablated PRG is statistically indistinguishable from GJR, confirming that the session-boundary information transfer is the sole driver of PRG's forecasting advantage. The Mincer--Zarnowitz $R^2$ drops from 0.464 to 0.264, indicating that the cross-session update explains nearly half of the variance captured by the full model.

\paragraph{Cross-market generalization.} We report the explicit ablation on SPY because it provides the longest continuous high-quality OHLC sample and a well-studied benchmark. However, the PRG-vs-Separate-GARCH comparisons in Table~\ref{tab:main_results} serve as the \emph{implicit} cross-market ablation: Separate GARCH uses identical session-specific parameterization but lacks the cross-session information bridge, so the PRG-vs-Separate gap isolates exactly the bridge effect. The Harvey-significant gaps span DM $t = -4.07$ (TAIFEX) to $-6.69$ (SPY) across all six markets, confirming that the SPY ablation result generalizes.

\subsection{Fair-information benchmark: GJR-X}
\label{sec:gjrx}

To isolate whether PRG's advantage stems from session-level information access or from the session-bridge architecture itself, we estimate a fair-information benchmark---GJR augmented with the lagged overnight squared return as an exogenous regressor (denoted GJR-X)---on the SPY out-of-sample window ($n=1{,}823$, 2019-01-02 to 2026-04-08). The conditional variance equation is
\begin{equation}
    h_t = \omega + \alpha\, r^2_{t-1} + \gamma\, \mathbb{1}(r_{t-1} < 0)\, r^2_{t-1} + \beta\, h_{t-1} + \delta\, r^2_{\text{overnight},t-1},
    \label{eq:gjrx}
\end{equation}
where $r^2_{\text{overnight},t-1}$ is the lagged overnight squared return. By construction, PRG and GJR-X consume identical session-level information; only the architecture differs.

Table~\ref{tab:gjrx} reports OOS QLIKE losses and the two K1260-specific Diebold--Mariano contrasts; the canonical PRG-vs-GJR comparison for SPY is reported in Table~\ref{tab:main_results} ($t = 6.00$). PRG Extended yields the lowest QLIKE (0.7559), followed by standard GJR (0.8544) and GJR-X (0.8607). The GJR-X-vs-GJR comparison yields DM $t = -0.53$ (not significant), indicating that the exogenous-regressor structure is not out-of-sample robust despite an in-sample log-likelihood ratio test that is decisive ($\hat{\delta} = 0.13$, $\mathrm{LR} = 49.37$, $p < 0.0001$ over 4{,}778 in-sample days, with 16 random initializations used in the LR diagnostic's internal grid search to verify basin stability). PRG-vs-GJR-X yields DM $t = 7.72$ ($p = 1.84 \times 10^{-14}$, Harvey PASS), the strongest single DM statistic in the paper. The asymmetry---in-sample significance for $\delta$ but no out-of-sample improvement, alongside PRG's decisive OOS dominance over GJR-X---demonstrates that PRG's advantage cannot be reduced to overnight information access. The session-level recursion architecture itself, not the information set, drives the forecasting gain.

%% 5. DISCUSSION

\section{Discussion}

The overnight variance share provides an intuitive explanation for the cross-market variation in PRG's advantage. EEM, with the highest overnight share (52.6\%), exhibits the largest DM statistic (6.63), while QQQ, with the lowest (27.9\%), shows the smallest (4.26). For GLD, the PRG Basic (without leverage) outperforms PRG Extended, consistent with the absence of leverage effects in commodities.

An important methodological consideration is that the PRG and GJR models operate at different observation frequencies: GJR uses one close-to-close return per day, while PRG uses two session-level returns (overnight and intraday). The PRG's advantage over GJR therefore reflects two distinct effects: (i) the information gain from observing opening prices, and (ii) the cross-session variance bridge. The comparison between PRG and Separate GARCH isolates the second effect, since both models use session-level data at the same frequency. The consistent Harvey-significant results for PRG versus Separate (DM $t = -4.07$ to $-6.69$) confirm that the cross-session bridge mechanism, not merely the higher observation frequency, drives the improvement. Open prices are freely available in all markets; the PRG's contribution is providing a principled framework for exploiting the information they contain.

The PRG simplifies the PRS model of \citet{Lai2024} by replacing Markov switching with a deterministic session index. The switching is observed, not latent, eliminating the Hamilton filter and avoiding well-known GARCH--Markov estimation difficulties \citep{Haas2004}. Compared to the coupled DCS-EGARCH of \citet{Linton2020} ($\sim$12 parameters) and the Overnight GARCH-It\^{o} of \citet{Kim2023} ($\sim$10 parameters requiring continuous-time estimation), the PRG achieves competitive or superior forecasting with 6--8 parameters and standard MLE.

Three limitations merit acknowledgment. First, the OHLC-based session decomposition uses squared returns rather than realized variance; tick-level validation for U.S. markets would strengthen the conclusions, though our TAIFEX tick-level analysis indicates that tick-level realized variance and the OHLC-based proxy yield quantitatively similar PRG estimates and the same DM $t$-ranking, mitigating this concern for markets with comparable session microstructure. Second, the VT strategy results do not account for transaction costs; the Table~\ref{tab:economic} footnote quantifies the expected drag at roughly 35--80 bps per annum under conservative TAIFEX execution assumptions, which preserves the PRG Extended ordering against all benchmarks and against buy-and-hold. Third, the fair-information GJR-X benchmark in Section~\ref{sec:gjrx} is currently estimated only for SPY; cross-asset GJR-X extension to the remaining five markets is left for follow-up work. The SPY result aligns with \citet{Todorova2014} and \citet{Opschoor2021}, who report that session-level parameterization dominates exogenous overnight regressors for realized variance and tail-risk forecasting.

%% 6. CONCLUSION
%% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

This paper introduces the Periodic Realized GARCH, a parsimonious model that captures session-specific volatility dynamics through a single GARCH recursion with periodic parameters. The key innovation is the cross-session information bridge: each session's variance forecast inherits information from the completed session's return, creating a natural conduit for overnight-to-intraday (and intraday-to-overnight) information flow.

Three findings stand out. First, the PRG significantly outperforms GJR-GARCH in all six markets tested, with DM $t$-statistics of 4.26--6.63, well above the Harvey (2016) threshold. Second, the ablation study demonstrates that removing the session-boundary update eliminates the entire advantage, establishing that periodic information transfer---not simply additional parameters---is the mechanism. Third, the fair comparison framework reveals that previously reported HAR dominance over GJR is substantially an artifact of target mismatch.

These results suggest that the overnight--intraday session boundary is a first-order feature of return dynamics that standard volatility models fail to exploit. The PRG provides a practical and effective way to incorporate this structure.

The PRG extends the Periodic Regime-Switching (PRS) model of \citet{Lai2024} by replacing its latent Markov-switching mechanism with an observed deterministic session index. This preserves the core PRS insight---that session-specific parameters capture empirically meaningful volatility asymmetries---while eliminating the estimation complexity and overfitting risks associated with regime-switching. The result is a simpler, more implementable specification for practitioners who want session-specific volatility dynamics without the computational and inferential overhead of latent-regime filtering.
